What is a Box & Whisker Plot?

What am I looking at?

It's a compact summary of how long a task usually takes. Instead of plotting every single entry, it shows where the bulk of the times fall and which ones stand out.

What's the box?

The box covers the middle 50% of the times - from the 25th percentile to the 75th (this span is called the IQR, the interquartile range). The vertical line inside the box is the median (the typical time), and the diamond marks the mean (the average). A wide box means people's times vary a lot; a narrow box means they're consistent.

What are the whiskers?

The lines extending from each side reach out to the normal range of times - up to 1.5× the box width beyond it. Think of them as "still within the expected spread."

What are the dots?

Those are outliers - individual times that fall outside the whiskers. They aren't necessarily errors; for a fast task they can just be the noticeably-longer-than-usual runs. Hover any dot to see who did it and on what date.

What are the dashed line and “r”?

What am I looking at?

Each dot is one person's day: how many items they handled (left to right) versus how many minutes the task took (bottom to top). Hover any dot to see who, when, and the exact numbers.

What's the dashed line?

The best-fit (trend) line - the single straight line that comes closest to all the dots at once. Its tilt is the useful part: it estimates how many extra minutes each additional item adds. That per-item rate is printed at the top right of the chart (e.g., +0.42 min/item).

What is “r”?

The correlation coefficient - a score from −1 to +1 for how tightly the dots hug the line. Near +1: more items reliably means more time. Near 0: no consistent relationship - something other than volume is driving the time. Negative values mean higher counts came with less time. Rough guide: 0.7+ is strong, 0.4-0.7 moderate, below 0.2 essentially noise.

Why should a manager care?

If r is strong, the item count is a good planning tool: multiply expected volume by the min/item rate and you have a time budget. If r is weak, don't staff off the counts - the time is coming from somewhere else (exceptions, system slowness, interruptions).

How is this data calculated?

How is this data calculated?

Where do the bars come from?

Straight off the Side Tasks Timing workbook. Every row there gives a person, a date, a task, and a start and stop time. Nothing is estimated or filled in - if a start/stop pair wasn't logged, that session simply isn't here.

What is “Average Day” actually averaging?

For each person and each task, it takes the average start time and the average stop time across every session in your current filter range, and draws one bar between them. So a bar reading 8:12 AM → 8:39 AM means “on a typical day this person starts that task around 8:12 and finishes around 8:39.” It is not any single real day - it's the shape of a normal day, assembled from all of them.

Is the bar length the average time on task?

Yes. Average stop minus average start works out to the same number as the average duration, so the width of each bar is the typical minutes spent. What you lose is the spread - a bar can't tell you whether that task is consistently 27 minutes or swings between 5 and 90. That's what the Box & Whisker chart below is for.

What counts toward the average?

Only sessions that survive the filters at the top - person, task group, and date range. Every logged session counts equally, so a task done 40 times and a task done twice both get a bar; the twice-done one is just far less trustworthy. Days a person didn't do a task don't drag the average down - they're absent, not zero.

Why do some tasks not show a bar?

Three reasons: the person never logged that task in the range; the row had no start/stop times; or the averaged stop landed at or before the averaged start, which is a data problem rather than a real schedule and gets dropped instead of drawn.

What about start/stop times entered wrong?

Times that land outside the 6:00 AM - 8:00 PM workday get their AM/PM flipped back into it before any averaging happens, since those are almost always a cell left as AM rather than real overnight work. Anything inside that window is left alone - 7:15 AM and 7:15 PM are both plausible, so neither is touched. Everything corrected is listed in the Anomalies panel with an AM/PM tag showing the original and the corrected times, so you can spot-check it.

And the SimpleAlloc row?

That's a different calculation. In Average Day it's an hourly matrix: each cell is the average number of SimpleAlloc events per active day in that hour - total events in the hour divided by the number of days that person actually worked in the range. Dividing by active days rather than calendar days keeps people with different coverage comparable. Switch off “Average Day” and it becomes real event bars for the single date you pick.
